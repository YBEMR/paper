       Abstract. With the widespread adoption of deep learning across indus-
       tries and academia, the energy consumption of AI computing clus-
       ters has surged, creating signiﬁcant economic and environmental chal-
       lenges. Improving AI server energy eﬃciency has therefore become a
       critical research focus. Traditional dynamic voltage and frequency scal-
       ing (DVFS) research has primarily focused on regulating either CPU or
       GPU/NPU frequency in isolation, which cannot fully unlock the energy-
       saving potential of AI servers. Naively applying joint DVFS requires
       exhaustively evaluating all frequency combinations to ﬁnd the most
       energy-eﬃcient one, which becomes prohibitively expensive with many
       frequency states.
          To address this limitation, this study proposes CoDVFS, a Coordi-
       nated Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling framework that employs
       Bayesian optimization to rapidly converge to optimal frequency conﬁgura-
       tions for both CPUs and GPU/NPUs. Experimental results on AI servers
       running HPL-MxP workloads demonstrate that CoDVFS enhances server
       energy eﬃciency by 17%, achieving signiﬁcant energy savings. We also
       analyze the relationship between whole-server and accelerator-only energy
       eﬃciency, proposing an optimum alignment criterion. On our device, the
       optima of both metrics coincide. However, the criterion reveals that if
       server and accelerator power scale diﬀerently on other devices, optimiz-
       ing accelerator eﬃciency alone may not reﬂect true server eﬃciency.

       Keywords: energy eﬃciency · DVFS · server

ing and computer vision have driven substantial demand for computational
resources. Research institutions and enterprises worldwide heavily invest in AI
clusters, relying on GPUs/NPUs (Neural Processing Units) for eﬃcient training
and inference. However, computational growth brings soaring energy costs. For
example, GPT-3 training consumed 1,287 MWh (502t CO2 , equal to 8 cars’
lifetime emissions). Computing facilities like Aurora peak at 38.7 MW [20],

potentially costing $30M/year and emitting 140,000t CO2 annually. As a result,
improving the energy eﬃciency of AI servers is now critical.
    Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling (DVFS) is an important technique
to control energy consumption and improve energy eﬃciency [1–5,8,10,12,14–
17,19,22,24,25,27–31]. By applying DVFS, we dynamically adjust a processor’s
operating frequency and supply voltage: increasing frequency and voltage under
heavy load to ensure performance, and lowering frequency and voltage under
light load to reduce power usage. This technique allows processors to minimize
energy consumption whenever possible.
    However, existing studies focus solely on applying DVFS to individual compo-
nents [1–5,8,10,12,14–17,19,22,24,25,27–31]—either the CPU or GPU—while
overlooking the fact that, in real tasks, the CPU and GPU work in coordina-
tion to complete computations. If DVFS is applied only to the CPU, the GPU
may still remain in a high-power state; likewise, optimizing only the GPU fre-
quency may result in CPU power consumption or execution performance failing
to match the GPU’s processing pace. Such isolated control strategies often fall
short in unlocking the full energy eﬃciency potential of an AI server.
    As a result, joint frequency and voltage scaling for both CPU and GPU
becomes important. Guided by this motivation, we propose CoDVFS, a Coordi-
nated Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling framework tailored for AI servers.
This framework can be applied across diﬀerent workloads and minimize power
consumption or maximize overall system energy eﬃciency by simultaneously
tuning the operating frequency and voltage of both the CPU and GPU.
    To facilitate optimization, we adopt a Bayesian optimization approach to
eﬃciently search for the optimal processor frequency combination, making the
control strategy more adaptive and precise.
    To validate the eﬀectiveness of CoDVFS, we conducted experiments on a
DGX server equipped with 8×GPU V100. We used external power monitor-
ing equipment to measure the energy consumption of the server while running
benchmarks. The experimental results show that, compared to the default DVFS
setting, CoDVFS improves server energy eﬃciency by 17% (HPL-MxP) and 12%
(HPCG), signiﬁcantly lowering overall electricity usage. Compared with DVFS
strategies applied only to CPU or GPU individually, the coordinated control
approach further exploits the system’s overall energy-saving potential.
    Since measuring full server power consumption is more challenging than
monitoring GPU power alone, prior GPU DVFS studies have typically focused
solely on GPU power measurements [1,3–5,8,12,14,16,22,24,25,28,30,31]. To
overcome this limitation, we present a detailed description of our methodology
for server-wide power measurement. More importantly, by comparing the GPU
power reported by the nvidia-smi tool with the total power measured by exter-
nal power meters, we conduct an in-depth analysis of the diﬀerences between
whole-server energy eﬃciency and GPU-only energy eﬃciency.
    Our analysis reveals that the point of optimal GPU energy eﬃciency does
not necessarily coincide with the point of optimal whole-server energy eﬃciency,
especially when the power consumption of other components, such as fans, mem-
ory, and the motherboard, varies diﬀerently with GPU frequency. We propose an

2   Background

Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling (DVFS) is a key technique used to
control the power consumption and performance of processors by adjusting
their operating voltage and frequency [1–5,8,10,12,14–17,19,22,24,25,27–31].
The dynamic power of a processor can typically be approximated by the for-
mula P ∝ CV 2 f [9], where C is the capacitance, V is the voltage, and f is the
frequency. This equation indicates that reducing the voltage and frequency leads
to a decrease in dynamic power consumption. Compared with other power man-
agement methods that require changes to application codes or hardware archi-
tecture, DVFS oﬀers several advantages: it is non-intrusive to user applications,
easily applicable, and provides fast response times as DVFS adjustment usually
ﬁnishes within 30 milliseconds [21]. As a result, DVFS is widely integrated into
modern CPUs and GPUs.
    Current CPUs and GPUs typically do not expose voltage control interfaces
to users. Instead, on-chip logic automatically adjusts the voltage based on the
conﬁgured frequency, and voltage scales approximately linearly with frequency.
In practice, this means that adjusting the processor’s operating frequency is
suﬃcient, with the corresponding voltage being managed internally by the pro-
cessor. The frequency-voltage curves can be found in [13,18]. In this study, we
regulate the frequencies of CPUs and GPUs, thereby indirectly managing their
supply voltages.
    Research on applying DVFS to CPUs has a long history. Numerous stud-
ies have explored how to achieve optimal power and performance trade-oﬀs
under various workloads and scenarios by adjusting CPU frequency and volt-
age [2,7,10,17,19,27,29]. This includes precise modeling of CPU power consump-
tion and the design of optimal frequency-setting strategies. With the widespread
adoption of multi-core CPU architectures, researchers have begun to investigate
the feasibility of applying diﬀerentiated frequency control to individual cores on
the same chip [19], as well as independent DVFS control of the uncore compo-
nents [10,27] (which include on-chip caches and memory controllers) to further
tap into energy-saving potential.
    Over the past decade, the widespread application of deep learning has driven
GPUs to the forefront of high-performance computing. Consequently, research
on GPU DVFS has also seen rapid growth [1,3–5,8,12,14–16,22,24,25,28,30,
31]. Many studies focus on modeling GPU performance and power consumption
under diﬀerent frequency settings, seeking to identify frequencies that strike a
422    Y. Zhang et al.

balance between energy eﬃciency and computational performance. For GPUs
that support memory frequency scaling, some research has also explored the
additional optimization opportunities that GPU memory DVFS can provide for
task execution [23].
    Most existing studies, however, focus on applying DVFS to either the CPU
or GPU in isolation and rarely explore the optimization potential of coordinated
CPU-GPU control. In addition, most prior experiments measured only the power
of the CPU or GPU chip itself, lacking a comprehensive analysis of whole-server
power usage. In fact, when server-level energy eﬃciency is assessed by using the
actual power of the entire server as the denominator, the resulting conclusions
may diﬀer from those drawn from GPU-only measurements.

energy eﬃciency beneﬁts of coordinated DVFS in data center servers. Table 1

3     CoDVFS: A Coordinated Dynamic Voltage
      and Frequency Scaling Framework

Existing frequency and voltage scaling techniques primarily target individual
computing components—either the CPU or GPU—for optimization. To address
this issue, it is essential to adopt a holistic perspective and perform coordinated
energy eﬃciency optimization across both the CPU and GPU.
    A straightforward way to implement joint CPU-GPU DVFS is to evalu-
ate all possible combinations of CPU and GPU frequencies exhaustively, and
then select the conﬁguration that yields the highest energy eﬃciency. However,
while exhaustive search is conceptually simple, it becomes prohibitively expen-
sive when the number of frequency states is large. Suppose the CPU has M
frequency states and the GPU has N states. If each combination is tested R
times to ensure measurement stability, then M × N ×R experiments are required
in total. For example, if M = N = 11 and R = 5, a total of 605 experiments
would be needed, which could take several hours or more. This time and resource
expenditure is impractical in AI computing environments.
    To overcome this limitation, we propose CoDVFS, a coordinated dynamic
voltage and frequency scaling framework for CPU-GPU systems. The core idea
of CoDVFS is to leverage Bayesian Optimization to eﬃciently approximate the
optimal solution in high-cost experimental environments. This allows the system
to discover the optimal CPU/GPU frequencies with few experiments.
    When using this framework, it can be applied to any target program requiring
optimization. CoDVFS will then automatically perform experimental evaluations
and iteratively identify the best frequency conﬁguration. The source codes of
CoDVFS are available at https://github.com/zyjopensource/CoDVFS.


3.1    Optimization Objective

The objective of the optimization process is to maximize the energy eﬃciency,
which is deﬁned as the ratio of performance to power usage:


                          Performance   Workload/Duration   Workload
      Energy Eﬃciency =               =                   =          .
                             Power       Energy/Duration     Energy

    According to this formula, when running the same workload (with a ﬁxed
computational demand), maximizing energy eﬃciency is equivalent to minimiz-
ing total energy consumption. During optimization on a server, performance,
power, and runtime duration are all measured directly while the program is
executing. Each experiment typically lasts tens of seconds to several minutes.
    The input variables for the optimization process are the combinations of
CPU and GPU frequencies (fCPU , fGPU ). Since most processor hardware auto-
matically adjusts voltage according to the selected frequency, it is suﬃcient to
directly set the frequencies externally.

3.2   Reason of Using Bayesian Optimization
Bayesian Optimization is a global optimization method based on probabilistic
models. It is particularly well-suited for optimizing functions that are expen-
sive or time-consuming to evaluate. In our case, because AI computing servers
are complex systems, the relationship between energy eﬃciency and CPU-GPU
frequency combinations cannot be described with an explicit analytical formula
unless complex modeling is done in advance. As a result, traditional methods
such as derivation-based or gradient-descent approaches are not applicable for
optimizing energy eﬃciency.
    Furthermore, since each experiment is relatively time-consuming, naive
brute-force search incurs a prohibitive computational cost. Bayesian Optimiza-
tion avoids the need for an analytical formula by constructing a probabilistic
model of the objective function. This model is then used to intelligently select
the next sampling point in the search space, allowing the optimization process
to eﬃciently approach the function’s optimum with relatively few experiments.

3.3   CoDVFS Framework Architecture
The proposed CoDVFS framework is implemented in Python and comprises key
modules: a Bayesian optimizer, workload runner, performance/power monitor-
ing, and CPU/GPU frequency control (Fig. 1).
    The Bayesian optimizer selects the next CPU-GPU frequency combination
using historical data and probabilistic modeling to maximize energy eﬃciency.
The workload runner executes the target program across frequency settings
for performance measurements. The performance monitoring module evaluates
workload execution based on program-reported metrics, and the power monitor-
ing module collects real-time power data from meters. Frequency control lever-
ages hardware-speciﬁc interfaces to adjust CPU/GPU frequencies.
    CoDVFS features adaptable interfaces for diﬀerent server setups, allowing
quick integration with new platforms by modifying frequency control calls. This
design ensures core optimization logic while enabling ﬂexible task execution,
monitoring, and coordinated CPU-GPU DVFS across servers, oﬀering a uniﬁed
solution for system-wide energy eﬃciency in real-world deployments.

3.4   Detailed Steps of Bayesian Optimization
Initialization of Sample Points. First, within the search space of CPU
and GPU frequencies, one or more initial frequency combinations are randomly
selected. For each combination, run the workload to collect performance/power
results and compute its energy eﬃciency.


Surrogate Model Construction. Using the data from the initial sample
points, a surrogate model is constructed to approximate the true objective func-
tion. A Gaussian Process (GP) is a commonly used surrogate model in Bayesian
Optimization. It characterizes the distribution of the objective function using a
mean function and a covariance function: f (x) ∼ GP(m(x), k(x, x )), where
x is the input variable (a CPU-GPU frequency combination (fCPU , fGPU )),
f (x) ∼ GP(·, ·) is the Gaussian process, m(x) is the mean function, k(x, x )
is the covariance function (also called the kernel), which describes the correla-
tion between inputs x and x .
    In Bayesian optimization, commonly used kernels include the RBF (Radial
Basis Function) kernel and the Matern kernel. Our implementation uses the
Matern kernel, which contains a few hyperparameters denoted as θ here.

Sampling Next Point. The core of Bayesian optimization lies in the sampling
of the next point. Based on the surrogate model, the next point is selected by
optimizing an acquisition function. The acquisition function evaluates the value
of each candidate point, which balances both exploration (gathering information
about unknown areas) and exploitation (reﬁning known good areas).
    Common acquisition functions include Expected Improvement (EI) and
Upper Conﬁdence Bound (UCB). Our implementation uses EI, which is:

Iterative Optimization and Convergence. Repeat the above sampling and
model updating steps, progressively searching for the optimal frequency combi-
nation. In each iteration, the updated model and acquisition function are used
to choose a new sampling point, gradually converging toward the optimum of
the objective function. After several iterations, when the value of the acquisition
function becomes suﬃciently small and the new sampling points oﬀer diminish-
ing improvements to the objective function, the optimization is considered to
have converged. At this point, the frequency combination returned by Bayesian
optimization is regarded as the optimal setting.

4     Experimental Metho dology
This section describes the details of the hardware, workloads, power measure-
ment, frequency control, and baseline DVFS policies used in our experiments.

4.1   Server and Workload
The server used in this study is a DGX V100, as shown in Fig. 2(a). It is equipped
with two Intel Xeon E5-2698 v4 CPUs, providing a total of 40 physical cores and
80 threads, along with eight V100 GPUs. The GPUs run on CUDA 10.2. Each
GPU consumes an idle power of ∼42 W, and the total idle power of all GPUs is
∼336 W. The entire server consumes ∼750 W in idle state.
428     Y. Zhang et al.

    To evaluate the eﬀectiveness of our proposed CoDVFS framework, we used
HPL-MxP [6] as the primary workload and ran it inside the oﬃcial NVIDIA
container. HPL-MxP is a representative hybrid-precision workload based on the
traditional HPL benchmark, designed to leverage both CPU and GPU comput-
ing capabilities. It evaluates both double-precision computation in HPC scenarios
and low-precision operations commonly found in AI workloads. In addition to
HPL-MxP, we also tested the HPCG benchmark [11], which solves large sparse
linear systems using iterative methods. We used the GPU version of these bench-
marks released on the NVIDIA GPU Cloud platform.
    After setting both the CPU and GPU set to their maximum frequencies, we
conducted a grid search over the tunable parameters N (matrix size) and N B
(block size) of HPL-MxP. This search is a standard procedure, which runs tests
to identify the conﬁguration that delivers the server’s peak performance. After
completing the grid search, we used matrix size N = 204800 and block size
N B = 896 that yielded the highest performance. These two parameters were
ﬁxed in all subsequent energy eﬃciency optimization experiments, ensuring a
fair comparison of energy performance across diﬀerent control strategies under
consistent workload conditions.

4.2    Server/GPU Power Measurement Method and Frequency
       Control Method

power meters (model GWGJ), as shown in Fig. 2(b). This power meter supports
TCP/IP-based communication via the SNMP protocol and can be controlled
through Python scripts with the pysnmp library, enabling remote real-time mon-
itoring and automated data logging. With a minimum valid sampling interval
of approximately 0.5 s, it is capable of capturing ﬂuctuations in dynamic server

4.3   Comparison Strategies

In our experiments, we compare the proposed CoDVFS framework with several
common DVFS strategies to validate its eﬀectiveness:

– Only-CPU-DVFS: This strategy applies DVFS only to the CPU, while the
  GPU remains ﬁxed at its maximum frequency.
– Only-GPU-DVFS: This strategy applies DVFS solely to the GPU, keeping
  the CPU ﬁxed at its maximum frequency.
430    Y. Zhang et al.

– MaxFreq: In this strategy, both the CPU and GPU operate at their maxi-
  mum frequencies. This is the default conﬁguration applied in many clusters
  with the aim of maximizing performance.
– MinFreq: This strategy sets both the CPU and GPU to their minimum
  frequencies. It helps measure the power control range of the server and is
  applicable in scenarios with strict power caps, such as when participating in
  electricity curtailment plans.

   Our experiments mainly compare metrics including energy eﬃciency, perfor-
mance, and average power. For performance, we directly read the ﬂoating-point
computation performance reported by HPL-MxP and HPCG.

In this section, we ﬁrst present the results from a coarse-grained DVFS brute-

5.1   Coarse-Grained CPU-GPU DVFS Brute-Force Search
To analyze the value of coordinated CPU-GPU DVFS, we ﬁrst performed a
coarse-grained brute-force search over CPU and GPU frequency combinations.
In the experiment, we selected 11 frequency points for the CPU, ranging from
1.2 GHz to 2.2 GHz, and 11 frequency points for the GPU, ranging from 135 MHz
to 1440 MHz. For each frequency pair, we ran the HPL-MxP workload ﬁve times,
and the average of the ﬁve runs was taken as the ﬁnal measurement value.
    Figure 3 shows the results of this exhaustive search: Fig. 3(a) presents the
measured performance of HPL-MxP in TFlops; Fig. 3(b) shows the average
server power in watts, measured using the external power meters; Fig. 3(c) shows
the energy eﬃciency in GFlops/W.
    From Figs. 3(a) and (b), we observe that as the CPU and GPU frequency
increases, both overall performance and power show a gradual increase. The high-
est performance and power are achieved when both CPU and GPU frequencies
are set near their maximum values. At this point, server power reaches 1702 W,
signiﬁcantly higher than the 911 W observed at the lowest frequency combina-
tion. Although in practical scenarios it is uncommon to set both CPU and GPU
to their minimum frequencies due to performance demands, this extreme case
demonstrates that coordinated DVFS can regulate server power consumption
across nearly a 50% range. This has practical signiﬁcance for computing clusters
whose total power is constrained, such as in power-capped environments.
    Compared with the monotonic increases observed in power and performance,
the energy eﬃciency results in Fig. 3(c) reveal more interesting patterns. Energy
eﬃciency does not continuously increase with rising frequency. Instead, it reaches
a peak value of 122.6 GFlops/W when the CPU frequency is set to 2.1 GHz and
the GPU frequency to 915 MHz. This peak highlights the potential advantage of
coordinated CPU-GPU DVFS.
    These results show that if DVFS is applied to only the CPU or only the
GPU, it may not discover this optimal frequency combination, and such single-
component strategies are unlikely to achieve a comparable level of energy eﬃ-
ciency. These ﬁndings also emphasize that to fully exploit the potential of coor-
dinated CPU-GPU control, it is essential for both the CPU and GPU to sup-
port a suﬃcient number of adjustable frequency levels. If only a small set of
DVFS options is available, optimization accuracy will be greatly reduced, and
the resulting energy eﬃciency may fall short of the theoretical optimum.

6     Discussion: Comparing Whole-Server Energy Eﬃciency
      and GPU Energy Eﬃciency

In previous work, it was often assumed by default that optimizing GPU energy
eﬃciency is equivalent to optimizing server energy eﬃciency. In this section, we
carefully analyze the diﬀerences between the two and provide a criterion for
establishing their equivalence.


6.1   The Distinction Between Two Metrics

In an AI computing server, components such as the CPU, GPU, motherboard,
fans, memory, storage, and network adapters collectively determine the total
server power consumption. Therefore, server-level energy eﬃciency is deﬁned
as the ratio of application performance to the total power consumption of all
components. In contrast, if we consider only the GPU, we are calculating the
ratio of performance to GPU power, resulting in the GPU energy eﬃciency.
    The distinction between the two can be clearly understood through the fol-
lowing formulas:
    GPU energy eﬃciency = (Performance)/(GPU power),
    Server energy eﬃciency = (Performance)/(GPU power + non-GPU power).
From the deﬁnition above, we also have:
    Server energy eﬃciency = GPU energy eﬃciency × GPU power ratio,
    GPU power ratio = (GPU power)/(GPU power + non-GPU power).
Clearly, when the GPU power ratio is constant, optimizing server energy eﬃ-
ciency is equivalent to optimizing GPU energy eﬃciency. However, in real-world
environments, the GPU power ratio varies dynamically, so we cannot directly
treat these two quantities as equivalent.
    In fact, during coordinated DVFS, the frequencies and voltages of both the
CPU and GPU are continuously adjusted, which not only aﬀects their individual
power consumption but also indirectly alters the power draw of other components
such as fans, memory, and the motherboard. This variability makes the GPU
power ratio no longer ﬁxed.
    In most prior research (see Table 1), due to experimental constraints, the
focus has been solely on GPU energy eﬃciency, using tools like nvidia-smi to
obtain GPU power data for analysis and optimization. However, comprehen-
sively evaluating whole-server energy eﬃciency requires external power meters
and power wiring reconﬁguration to measure total consumption, which is often
infeasible in real data centers. Consequently, research on whole-server energy
eﬃciency has been relatively limited.
    The problem of focusing on GPU energy eﬃciency is that the optimal point
for GPU energy eﬃciency does not always coincide with the optimal point for
server energy eﬃciency. Although the discrepancy was small in our experiments,
there is no guarantee that the alignment of the two optimal points will hold true
under diﬀerent hardware conﬁgurations and workloads.

have the following Optimum Alignment Criterion: if at the GPU’s energy
eﬃciency optimal point, the relative growth rate of GPU power is equal to the
relative growth rate of non-GPU power, then this point is also the server’s energy

performance and non-GPU power begin to decline, while the growth rate of
GPU power increases rapidly, which is consistent with our criterion.
    We suspect that the operational behavior of the server’s cooling fans explains
why non-GPU power initially increases signiﬁcantly and later stabilizes as GPU
frequency increases. In our experiment, the CPU frequency was ﬁxed at 2.1 GHz,
meaning CPU-side power remained relatively stable. However, as the GPU fre-
quency rose, system heat output increased sharply, triggering an increase in fan
speed, which in turn boosted non-GPU component power.
    Once the fans approached their maximum speed, further increases in GPU
frequency no longer caused fan power increases, leading to a plateau in non-GPU
power, as shown by the green line in Fig. 5(c). This power trend is relatively
common in servers using air-cooling technologies. If alternative cooling methods
such as liquid cooling are adopted, the impact of the fans may be signiﬁcantly
reduced, which could also change the overall trend of non-GPU power. In such
cases, the deviation between GPU energy eﬃciency and server energy eﬃciency
may increase, necessitating further in-depth comparative study.

    In summary, when comparing GPU and server energy eﬃciency, their two
optimal points do not necessarily align under DVFS control, due to the non-
constant GPU power ratio. However, in the workload and hardware conﬁguration
used in our experiment, the two optima were closely aligned, which validates
the feasibility of using GPU energy eﬃciency (which is easier to measure) to
approximate whole-server energy eﬃciency.
    At the same time, it’s important to recognize that in other setups, such as
liquid-cooled servers, cooling power characteristics may diﬀer signiﬁcantly. This
could widen the gap between GPU and server energy eﬃciency, underscoring
the need for further study. Deeper understanding of this issue will contribute to
more accurate system-level energy eﬃciency evaluation and optimization, and
provide new insights for future research in green computing and thermal design.

7   Conclusion and Future Work
This study proposes CoDVFS, a CPU-GPU joint frequency control framework
for AI server energy eﬃciency. Experiments on a DGX V100 show Bayesian opti-
mization eﬀectively ﬁnds optimal frequency combinations, achieving 17% higher
energy eﬃciency than default settings under HPL-MxP workloads. While GPU-
only and whole-server optima show minor diﬀerences, GPU eﬃciency serves as
a reliable proxy. Future work includes ﬁner-grained DVFS for multi-stage work-
loads, multi-task CPU-GPU coordination, and revisiting GPU-system eﬃciency
alignment under liquid cooling.
