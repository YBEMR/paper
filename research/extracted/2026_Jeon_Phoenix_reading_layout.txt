Running multiple deep neural networks (DNNs) simultaneously on mobile devices introduces challenges due
to constrained computing resources. Previous research has explored the use of heterogeneous processors for
accelerating DNN inference but often overlooks thermal issues, which can degrade computing power. In this
article, we propose Phoenix, a system specifically designed to enhance the performance of multi-instance
DNNs in video applications by maximizing accuracy and ensuring the achievement of a required frame rate.
Phoenix allocates DNN tasks to the most suitable hardware processors, understanding complex thermal dy-
namics through reinforcement learning, and postpones the onset of thermal throttling. Despite optimized
task allocation, continuous inference of multiple DNNs can still lead to thermal throttling. To manage per-
formance degradation, Phoenix employs a multi-exit network, adaptively executing inference tasks to ensure
consistent frame rates. Phoenix minimizes accuracy loss from early exits by optimally generating and op-
erating multi-exit networks. We evaluated Phoenix using two different benchmarks and Virtual Youtuber
streaming application. The results demonstrated that Phoenix effectively enhances device performance by
delaying thermal throttling and achieving optimal accuracy while maintaining a consistent frame rate.

1   Introduction
On-device artificial intelligence (AI) is gaining popularity for its benefits in privacy, real-time
responses, and reduced cloud costs. Notably, many emerging immersive applications such as vir-
tual YouTubers (VTuber) [1] and virtual and augmented reality [2–4] require the execution of
multi-instance deep neural networks (DNNs) within a single app. However, the limited computing
resources of mobile devices make running such computationally intensive tasks challenging.
   The use of heterogeneous mobile processors offers a viable solution for accelerating multi-
instance DNN inference by running tasks across different processors to reduce overall latency.
The key to this approach lies in matching each DNN task with the best-fit processor, consider-
ing the diverse specifications of processors and DNNs. Many studies have shown that leveraging
heterogeneous mobile processors could speed up inference [5–7]; however, heat generation poses
practical challenges. Mobile device performance is highly influenced by dynamic thermal condi-
tions [8–10], yet there has been limited focus on addressing thermal issues in multi-instance DNN
operations. Furthermore, many video applications require a specific frame rate for a smooth user
experience, making it crucial to manage thermal challenges to maintain consistent performance.
   In long-running, frame-driven video pipelines, thermal throttling introduces two key challenges
that must be addressed to ensure a smooth and consistent user experience in multi-instance DNN
workloads on mobile devices. First, when DNN tasks are assigned without considering the thermal
sensitivities of heterogeneous processors, thermally weaker processors can overheat and throttle
earlier. This not only shortens the high-performance period but also accelerates frame drops and
latency spikes. Our preliminary results (Section 2.2) show that the point at which FPS violations
begin can differ by up to 2 × depending solely on the task allocation policy—even under identical
workloads. Second, even with thermally optimized task assignment, sustained execution of mul-
tiple DNNs inevitably produces excessive heat that exceeds the device’s Thermal Design Power
(TDP) [11]. Once thermal throttling begins, computing power drops non-uniformly across pro-
cessors, as the timing and severity of slowdown vary depending on each processor’s architecture
and thermal inertia. Without an adaptive strategy that responds to these changes in real time, the
system fails to maintain target frame rates, degrading user experience.
   In this article, we propose Phoenix, a thermal-aware multi-instance inference system that
co-optimizes task placement across heterogeneous processors and adaptive early-exit inference,
to maintain sustained real-time performance under thermal throttling. First, Phoenix learns a
thermal-aware task allocation policy using reinforcement learning (RL) [12, 13]. By observing
how different configurations affect heat generation and overall latency, the RL agent learns to map
each DNN task to the most suitable processor to postpone throttling as much as possible. Second,
Phoenix employs a multi-exit network [14, 15], which includes several early exits along the depth
of the network. By analyzing the slowdown patterns caused by thermal throttling, Phoenix con-
figures an optimal set of exit points to maximize accuracy while maintaining stable inference per-
formance under thermal fluctuations. Lastly, to achieve optimal accuracy while meeting real-time
constraints, Phoenix offers two types of runtime schedulers: a best-effort scheduler that selects the
most accurate exit based on the current operating condition, and a sustained performance sched-
uler that proactively adjusts exit depth to maintain stable performance over prolonged execution
under thermal stress. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

2 Motivation
2.1 Performance Effects on Task Allocation
Previous studies have demonstrated that running DNNs on mobile devices can induce thermal
throttling [10, 16, 17]. However, there has been limited exploration into the influence of execution
configurations on heat generation. To investigate the performance degradation patterns depend-
ing on execution configurations, we evaluated the Samsung Galaxy S22 running four different
DNNs listed in Table 1. Each DNN task can be explicitly assigned to a specific processor, such as
the CP U, GP U, or other accelerator like DSPs. When running on the CPU, the number of threads
can be specified. For the accelerators, preference options such as FastSingleAnswer (FSA), Sus-
tainedSpeed (SS) or LowPower (LP) are selectable through NNAPI delegation [18]. We allocate
each DNN task according to two different allocation policies as shown in Table 1. We then log the
application’s FPS, the device’s temperature, and the clock frequencies of both CP U and GP U. The
latency constraints are set to 50 milliseconds to ensure the application should run at 20 FPS.
   Figure 1 shows the experiment results from two different policies. As illustrated in Figure 1(a),
with Config. 1, the CPU frequencies start to decrease immediately after the application starts run-
ning, and the GPU frequency starts to drop at about 180 seconds. Due to this performance degrada-
tion, the application fails to meet the given latency constraints starting from 61 seconds. Following
this, from approximately 280 seconds, it operates at about 13.5 FPS, and then, following a further
drop in the CPU’s big core frequency at 520 seconds, it operates at around 12.5 FPS. In the case of
Config. 2, thermal throttling on the CPU occurs after 150 seconds, and the GPU after 200 seconds.
As the GPU’s performance degrades, the frame rate drops below 20 FPS and fails to meet the la-
tency constraints. The frame rate decreases during the decline of GPU frequency, and eventually
converges to 11.6 FPS at 310 seconds.
   From the experiment results, we can draw the following insights. Allocating each task to the
most suitable processor helps delay thermal throttling and maintain system performance. By dy-
namically adjusting task allocation based on the current thermal condition, it is possible to min-
imize latency violations while extending stable operation. Still, continuous DNN inference in-
creases heat accumulation, which can eventually lead to thermal throttling. This underscores the

2.2    Slowdown Pattern of Mobile Processor
Mobile processors comprise various types, each with distinct performance characteristics [19, 20].
We analyzed how performance degradation varies among each processor under thermal throttling
conditions. We ran the application for 15 minutes using Config. 2 as described in Section 2.1 and
logged the execution time of each DNN. Figure 2 presents a graph showing the distribution of
the slowdown during the experiment for each processor. To compare the slowdown of each pro-
cessor, we normalize the execution latency by dividing the execution time by the time without
thermal throttling. A value of 1 represents the optimal case, while a value of 2 indicates the exe-
cution time has doubled. This graph provides two key insights. First, there exists a predominant
slowdown range for each processor. For instance, the GPU, operating at frequencies of 640 MHz
and 285 MHz, experiences a latency increase by approximately 1.2 to 2.3 times. This implies that
each DVFS-enabled processor operates at certain frequencies most of the time. Second, the degree
of slowdown varies across processors. This variation is due to each processor being designed for
different purposes and having different frequency ranges. Thus, to provide a seamless user experi-
ence using heterogeneous mobile processors, we should carefully consider the different slowdown
patterns of each processor.

2.3    Multi-Exit Network Inference
A multi-exit network is a model that incorporates multiple early exits at different depths, leverag-
ing the tradeoff between DNN task accuracy and computational cost to mitigate latency violations.
This characteristic makes it a promising option for addressing thermal issues in on-device DNN
inference. However, existing research has rarely explored how the early exiting mechanism can
be utilized to manage thermal throttling. To investigate this, we examined how multi-exit net-
works help delay thermal throttling and manage its effects when it occurs. We allocated DNN
tasks as Config. 2, inserted two early exits into each DNN, and performed continuous DNN infer-
ence. Figure 3 shows the FPS traces over 15 minutes using different exits, where ‘#0’ denotes an
exit point in the backbone network, and higher exit numbers indicate shallower exits.
   As shown in Figure 3, using a shallower exit significantly delays the onset of thermal throttling.
When executing inference with exit #0, FPS starts to drop at 218 seconds, whereas #1 delays this
drop until 392 seconds, and #2 maintains a stable FPS throughout the entire period. Additionally,

4   Task Allocation Policy
4.1 RL-Based Task Allocation
Phoenix system first assigns k different DNN inference tasks to n different execution options. As
observed in the experiments in Section 2.1, the execution time for multi-DNNs inference and the
onset of thermal throttling vary depending on the execution configuration of each DNN.
   The Need for DQN-based RL. Many algorithms are candidates for constructing the best task
allocation policy. One could hand-craft a rule set to select execution options for multi-DNN infer-
ence. However, in practice, such rules are brittle for multi-DNN workloads: the set of concurrently
running DNNs can vary across applications. As a result, the scheduler must account for a large
number of task-combination cases and their shared-resource interference. A brute-force approach,
which tests all task-execution combinations, is one option, but it becomes impractical as the search
space grows exponentially with nk . Alternatively, a simple workload-driven heuristic—such as as-
signing heavier tasks to more powerful hardware processors—can provide a practical baseline,
but it cannot guarantee optimality across diverse task combinations and device conditions. More
importantly, such heuristics cannot explicitly account for long-term thermal effects.
   A learning-based approach provides a more robust solution than hand-crafted rules, because it
can adapt to unknown device conditions and generalize across diverse multi-DNN combinations.
Surrogate-guided supervised learning is possible, but it hinges on accurate label/oracle genera-
tion under delayed, coupled thermal effects. In particular, RL is well-suited to sequential decision-
making problems [12, 13] where each action must be chosen based on the current device state
while accounting for long-term consequences such as thermal accumulation and delayed throt-
tling. In our setting, the scheduler observes runtime states (e.g., performance slowdown trends
and thermal headroom) and selects an inference configuration for each DNN; it then improves its
policy by receiving reward feedback that reflects both performance and thermal outcomes.

   To evaluate the value of an action, RL methods range from tabular Q-learning [21, 22] to incor-
porating DNNs [23, 24]. Tabular Q-learning stores Q-values for each discretized state–action pair
and can be effective when the environment can be enumerated reliably. However, Phoenix runs
in dynamic mobile settings where runtime signals and multi-DNN interference vary over time, so
the scheduler often faces unseen states. In such settings, the key requirement is to approximate
Q (s , a) in a way that generalizes, especially near throttling/FPS-violation boundaries with delayed
thermal effects. Coarse discretization may miss boundary effects, whereas fine discretization is
hard to reuse across conditions. Phoenix therefore uses a DQN to approximate Q (s , a), allowing
the policy to learn nonlinear signal–outcome relationships and generalize beyond visited states.
   Method Overview. Phoenix learns task-allocation policies offline and applies them with min-
imal overhead at runtime. During training, each DNN is managed by a dedicated DQN agent. The
agent observes device-level signals that capture shared-resource contention and thermal condi-
tions, and updates its policy from collected transition tuples. To avoid the constant overhead of
online DQN inference, Phoenix deploys a lightweight rule table derived from the learned policy.
The table is indexed by the CP U/GP U operating-frequency pair and enables fast, predictable run-
time selection of each DNN’s execution configuration. Note that the deployed rule table is not a
Q-table over the full RL state. Instead, it is a compact, deployment-oriented lookup keyed only by
low-overhead runtime signals, namely the selected CP U/GP U frequency pair, which allows deter-
ministic and constant-time decisions. In contrast, the RL training state includes continuous, high-
cardinality signals such as per-processor slowdown trends, thermal headroom, and contention
indicators across concurrently running DNNs. Building a classic Q-table for Q (s , a) would there-
fore require discretizing multiple continuous dimensions and their cross-products, yielding either
an impractically large table or overly coarse bins. Coarse bins are particularly harmful around
throttling and FPS-violation boundaries, where small changes in headroom or slowdown can flip
the correct action, while fine bins lead to sparse visitation and poor reuse when the active DNN
set and interference patterns change.

5     Multi-Exit Network Configuration
5.1 Need of Optimal Early-Exit Architecture
The task allocation method can minimize heat generation and delay thermal throttling; however,
continuously executing DNNs inference can still cause throttling [10, 16, 17]. When thermal throt-
tling occurs, device performance degrades, extending execution time of DNN inference and leading
to a decrease in the FPS. Phoenix copes with the frame drop problem by employing multi-exit net-
works [14, 15, 25, 26] which balance accuracy and latency by adaptively determining exit points.
   Specifically, the neural architecture of the early-exit determines the computational demand and
accuracy while early-exiting [14]. Designing early-exit networks involves deciding the number of
exits, placing each exit, and configuring the architecture of each exit. Experimental results from
Section 2.2 show varying performance degradation patterns across mobile processors, emphasiz-
ing the need of careful consideration in exit design. To this end, we design neural architecture
search (NAS) system [27, 28] considering the performance degradation pattern of each processor.
   First, we define search space for early-exits based on the architectural characteristics of multi-
exit network. Since each DNN has a unique architecture, Phoenix allows users to input a cus-
tomized search space to accommodate the specific structures of their DNNs. Next, using a
hardware-in-the-loop approach, we set the smartphone’s CP U/GP U frequencies and measure the
execution time of early-exits under thermal throttling conditions. Phoenix then identifies the op-
timal neural architecture that maximizes accuracy while adhering to latency constraints.

5.2     Search Space
The search space for early-exit networks must include the number, placement, and structure of
early exits.
    Number. Having too many early exits can potentially degrade the overall performance of the
network by negatively impacting the convergence during training [14, 29]; hence, it is crucial to
determine an adequate number of exits. Our preliminary experiments show that while mobile pro-
cessors offer a wide range of frequencies, the frequency options used for extended periods are lim-
ited. Based on this observation, we configured the number of exits as the number of deployed rules.
For example, if the "Deploying" function of Algorithm 1 outputs three different keys—frequency
pairs for CPU and GPU—each network can have a maximum of three exits. In some cases, networks
may meet the target latency without needing early exits, and such scenarios are not included in
the count of early exit networks.
    Location and Early-exit Architecture. Recent neural networks consist of multiple consec-
utive blocks, with each block containing several layers. We configured the search space to allow
early exits to be selectively positioned after any block. However, running the backbone network
under thermal throttling conditions may result in failure to meet the given latency requirements
if inference proceeds beyond a certain block. Based on this, we excluded blocks that do not meet
latency criteria from the search space when determining exit locations.

   Meanwhile, since each network has a different structure, defining a general search space for exit

5.3    Evaluation Function and Searching
The objective function is calculated based on the fulfillment of specified latency requirements by
the given early exits and their impact on accuracy. Measuring accuracy is straightforward using a
validation dataset. To accurately obtain the execution time of the DNN inference task, we exploit
the hardware-in-the-loop approach. After training the discovered early-exit network on a remote
server, we deploy the model on a mobile device. Our NAS system then sets the CPU and GPU
frequencies considering thermal throttling and measures the execution time.
   The goal of NAS is to search for the optimal multi-exit architecture that achieves the highest
accuracy while adhering to latency constraints under limited frequencies. However, when more
than two DNNs run on specific processors, the execution of DNNs inference must be completed
within Lcons . In this case, we distribute the latency budget based on the execution time proportion
of each DNN’s backbone network, allowing users to adjust this ratio as needed. The goal is then

6     Runtime Scheduler
The runtime scheduler is composed of two key components, task allocation and exit selection,
each relying on outputs from Sections 4 and 5, respectively. The TaskAlloc function in Algo-

GPU frequencies using a moving average to account for temporary frequency boosts due to touch
interactions (line 2). It then determines the closest frequency set based on the Euclidean distance
from the current frequencies (line 3) and assigns each DNN task accordingly (lines 4–5).
   For exit selection, we introduce two scheduling strategies: best-effort and sustained perfor-
mance. The best-effort method maximizes accuracy at each instance, as described in the BestEffort
function in Algorithm 2. In an ideal scenario, the multi-exit network obtained would allow select-
ing the best exit based on the current operating frequency. However, in real-world, factors such as
background applications and touch boosts can introduce fluctuations in DNN inference latency.
To prevent such potential latency violations, Phoenix checks whether the execution latency of
each DNN satisfies L app at every inference cycle. If the measured execution time exceeds the la-

performance.

9 Related Works
9.1 Thermal Management Techniques
Many studies have explored solutions to keep devices within safe thermal limits by dynamically
adjusting processor frequencies, using methods such as modeling-based method [38, 39], feed-
back control theory [40], and machine learning [11]. For example, zTT proposed a DRL-based
algorithm that optimizes the operating frequency of both the CPU and GPU to manage thermal
conditions. These DVFS-centric approaches are largely reactive, in that they regulate frequen-
cies after thermal pressure emerges, and thus often struggle to sustain stable application-level
latency/FPS once throttling persists. Consequently, they are limited in maintaining consistent FPS
or user experience once throttling occurs. Complementary to DVFS control, THERMOS [41] ex-
plores thermal-aware RL scheduling on heterogeneous multi-chiplet PIM architectures, optimiz-
ing execution time–energy tradeoffs under thermal constraints. However, THERMOS does not
address sustained mobile workloads where throttling progressively reduces compute availability,
nor does it incorporate accuracy/latency adaptation via early-exit inference. In contrast, Phoenix
proactively manages the thermal state by optimizing DNN task placement before heat accumula-
tion reaches critical levels, and further mitigates post-throttling performance degradation through
adaptive multi-exit inference.

9.2 Heterogeneous Mobile Processors
Heterogeneous mobile processors offer an opportunity to accelerate DNN inference on resource-
constrained mobile devices through concurrent DNN execution. One approach is to accelerate a

                        ACM Trans. Embedd. Comput. Syst., Vol. 25, No. 2, Article 31. Publication date: March 2026.
31:20                                                                                         S. Jeon et al.

single DNN inference by dividing the network into sub-graphs and executing these sub-graphs on
different processors concurrently [38, 39]. However, these methods incur communication overhead
while synchronizing the outputs from each processor. NN-stretch [42] was proposed to address
this issue, yet it faces the limitation of not accounting for the varying computing capacities due
to thermal throttling. Another approach is running multiple DNNs on multiple processors. Band
[5] introduced a model analyzer and scheduler specifically designed to manage the workload of
multiple DNNs on a heterogeneous mobile processor. However, this approach necessitates mod-
ifications to the code of deep learning frameworks, and it does not thoroughly consider thermal
issues in the design of the inference system. Sung et al. [6] proposed a DAAS for scheduling mul-
tiple DNNs on mobile processors without framework modification. Yet, as our evaluation shows,
their policy performs sub-optimally in sustained workloads where thermal throttling significantly
alters device behavior. Surrogate-guided supervised learning methods [43, 44] have also been used
for task allocation on heterogeneous mobile processors. However, under thermal throttling with
multi-instance interference, surrogate-based labeling becomes unreliable for capturing long-term
thermal effects, so we instead learn a policy via RL. Overall, existing heterogeneous scheduling
methods optimize for latency or energy under the assumption of static processor performance. In
contrast, Phoenix explicitly accounts for thermally-induced performance variations in multi-DNN
settings, enabling sustainable long-term operation.

9.3     Multi-Exit Network
Recent works have attempted to mitigate performance degradation by designing multi-exit net-
works that consider the changing computing capacity according to the frequencies of mobile pro-
cessors. Dělen [45] proposed an adaptive model-serving system for the on-device execution of
multi-instance DNNs to maximize user experience. However, Dělen did not consider the neural
architecture which can significantly impact the overall performance of on-device applications.
Furthermore, Dělen’s approach schedules tasks using only GPUs, thereby failing to leverage the
advantages of heterogeneous mobile processors. Meanwhile, NAS has been extensively used to ob-
tain optimal neural architectures. For instance, OmniLive [37] optimized a multi-exit network for
video super resolution by exploring various early-exit networks based on the lowest and highest
mobile GPU frequencies. HADAS [46] simultaneously optimized both the structure of the multi-
exit network and the DVFS policy for energy-efficient DNN inference. However, to the best of our
knowledge, none of the existing studies have thoroughly considered thermal throttling patterns
in the design of neural architectures. Moreover, previous works often overlook the importance of
selecting the appropriate number of exit networks, which can impact overall performance.

10 Discussion
We discuss issues related to the proposed approach and solutions. One may question whether this
approach can be generalized to other types of devices and operating systems. Phoenix leverages
learning-based solutions, NAS, and an RL-based scheduler to optimize the operation of multi-
instance DNNs on mobile devices. Unlike rule-based solutions, learning-based solutions can adapt
to unknown environments. For example, given a backbone network, NAS adjusts resource con-
straints specific to each device, enabling it to select the best early-exit networks. Next, recent
efforts have explored RL-based scheduling and multi-exit network design to optimize inference
systems. Phoenix differs in that it explicitly targets multi-instance real-time inference under ther-
mal throttling, where compute availability varies over time and across processors. Phoenix pro-
vides an end-to-end scheme that jointly optimizes task allocation and adaptive early-exit inference
using real-time workload and device-state observations to sustain FPS/QoE beyond throttling. To

11    Conclusion
Video applications leveraging multiple concurrent DNNs are becoming increasingly common on
mobile devices, making heat management critical for sustaining performance. In this article, we
proposed Phoenix, the first thermal-aware multi-DNN inference system that proactively optimizes
task allocation and network configuration to prevent thermal throttling. Unlike traditional reactive
approaches based on DVFS, Phoenix anticipates thermal dynamics to maintain consistent infer-
ence quality over time. We plan to extend this work to running multiple DNNs on a wider range
of devices, for instance, AR/VR goggles. These devices will execute various DNNs concurrently,
including gaze tracking and hand pose estimation. By exploiting both the device and edge server,
we expect that it will maximize the user experience of the target applications.
