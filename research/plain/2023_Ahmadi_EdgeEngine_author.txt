ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Heterogeneous edge platforms enable the efficient execution of machine learning inference applications. These applications often have
a critical constraint (such as meeting a deadline) and an optimization goal (such as minimizing energy consumption). To navigate
this space, existing optimization frameworks adjust the platform’s
frequency configuration for the CPU, the GPU and/or the memory controller. However, existing optimization frameworks have
two limitations. First, edge applications are frequently deployed
in environments where they are exposed to ambient temperature
variations. Second, a recent study has shown that temperature
has a significant impact on edge platform characteristics. In this
context, today’s frequency optimization frameworks (which are
thermal-oblivious) select frequency configurations that either violate the application’s constraints, or are sub-optimal in terms of the
optimization goal.
To address these shortcomings, we propose EdgeEngine, a lightweight thermal-aware optimization framework. EdgeEngine monitors the platform’s temperature and uses reinforcement learning to adjust the frequency configuration of all underlying platform resources to meet the application’s constraints. We find that
EdgeEngine meets the application’s constraint, and achieves 29%
lower energy consumption (up to 2x) and 41% fewer violations compared to existing state-of-the-art thermal-oblivious optimization
frameworks.

Context. Recent advances in machine learning (ML) research have
led to numerous applications (e.g., computer vision, autonomous
driving) that are deployed on resource-constrained edge platforms
(e.g., drones, autonomous vehicles, IoT devices) [19, 33]. These applications usually have specific Quality of Service (QoS) objectives
(e.g., latency, energy budget, accuracy) for the target inference tasks.
Violating these objectives can have severe consequences [14], e.g.,
an autonomous vehicle colliding with an obstacle due to missing
an object detection deadline, a drone crashing after running out of
battery.
At the same time, the advent of heterogeneous embedded edge
platforms with a System on Chip (SoC) design that integrate multiple processing units (e.g., CPU, GPU) on the same chip, such as
the NVIDIA Jetson platform family, has enabled the execution of
ML inference workloads at the edge [14, 16]. These platforms are
designed to speed up ML-related tasks in a power-efficient manner,
and employ tunable processing units and memory components
[3, 5].
Frequency optimization frameworks [7, 8, 10, 11, 14, 17, 31] (§3.3)
have the important role of adjusting the frequency configuration of
the underlying components of the edge platform aiming to satisfy
application constraints while simultaneously optimizing one or
more unconstrained dimensions, e.g., meet a target deadline while
minimizing energy consumption.
Problem Statement. A significant challenge for such optimization frameworks is that edge devices are often deployed in uncontrolled environments with high temperature variations. For
example, temperatures in the desert can vary from below 0°C at
night to 70°C when exposed directly to the sun [13]. Our work
(summarized in §2, [6]) has found that there is significant variation
in edge platforms’ power consumption (up to 40%) and running
time (to 5%) when the ambient temperature changes from 25°C to
70°C. Unfortunately, existing frequency optimization frameworks
are thermal-oblivious, i.e., their design does not consider the ambient temperature. As a result, these frameworks either provide
sub-optimal solutions or, more importantly, fail to meet their QoS
objectives (§3.2 and §3.3). To tackle the challenge posed by ambient
temperature fluctuations, optimization frameworks must adapt in
real-time. The adaptation should be: (i) swift, ensuring timely adjustments to changing temperatures, and (ii) conservative, minimizing
the number of violations during the adaptation.
However, the large frequency configuration space of current
heterogeneous edge platforms poses a challenge for achieving realtime adaptation, and identifying efficient configurations. This is
influenced by two factors. First, these platforms have numerous
components that are frequency configurable, namely the processing units (e.g., CPU, GPU, application-specific accelerators) as well

■ Proposing, EdgeEngine, a thermal-aware optimization framework
based on RL (§4). EdgeEngine supports both deadline- and energyconstrained applications (§5.5). EdgeEngine incorporates thermal awareness and leverages RL and several domain-specific
heuristics to enable rapid adaptation with minimal violations in
response to temperature changes. Importantly, EdgeEngine is
capable of adjusting the frequency for all the underlying components to identify the most efficient settings. Finally, EdgeEngine
is robust to variability during inference execution, ensuring compliance with the application’s constraints (§6.2).
■ Evaluating EdgeEngine on various workloads, constraints, and
environments as well as comparing it to related work (§6). We
design and implement an emulation environment (§5.4 to systematically expose the platform to different ambient temperatures, while running various ML inference workloads with both

NeuOS [7] and AutoScale [17], with TOF (a thermal-oblivious
variant of EdgeEngine), as well as with a perfect-knowledge ORACLE (that always selects the optimal configuration) in terms of (i)
satisfying the application constraint, (ii) optimization goal - energy efficiency (energy per inference) for deadline-constrained
applications, and runtime (i.e., inference execution time) for
energy-constrained applications.
We demonstrate that EdgeEngine adapts successfully in settings that have energy or runtime constraints. When compared
to existing state-of-the-art thermal-oblivious optimization frameworks, EdgeEngine demonstrates significant improvements. It
achieves an average of 29% lower energy consumption and 26%
fewer violations for deadline-constrained applications, as well
as an average of 8% lower runtime and 41% fewer violations for
energy-constrained applications. Notably, EdgeEngine’s selected
configurations incur only a slight increase of up to 3% in runtime and energy consumption compared to those selected by the
perfect knowledge ORACLE (and lead to no violations).
Finally, the lightweight version of reinforcement learning
used (Q-Learning) leads to low performance and memory overheads thus making EdgeEngine well-suited for deployment on
computationally-constrained embedded devices.

Objectives. To address this issue, this study explores the design
of a practical thermal-aware frequency optimization framework
for edge inference that aims to satisfy the following six objectives: (𝑂 1 ) Adaptation to platform temperature: ability to adjust
the system’s frequency configuration based on the current platform
temperature; (𝑂 2 ) Cover the full configuration space: able to select
energy/performance efficient configuration by adjusting frequencies for all the underlying components (e.g., CPU, GPU, memory
controller); (𝑂 3 ) Real-time adaptation: ability to swiftly adapt with a
low number of violations during the adaptation process; (𝑂 4 ) Robust
to noise: ability to handle the inherent variability in execution time
and power consumption during application execution; (𝑂 5 ) Lightweight design: low resource overheads such that it can be deployed
on resource-constrained edge devices; and (𝑂 6 ) Generalizability:
ability to be deployed in different environments and support a large
class of inference applications.
Approach. To this end, we propose EdgeEngine1 , a lightweight
thermal-aware optimization framework for real-time inference on
heterogeneous edge devices. EdgeEngine configures the platform’s
CGM2 frequency configuration to efficiently satisfy the applications’ constraints and optimization objectives in response to variations in platform temperature.
In EdgeEngine, users specify the application’s constraints in the
energy or time dimensions (i.e., an energy budget or target deadline)
and an optimization objective. At execution time, EdgeEngine continuously observes the platform’s temperature and the application
behavior, and selects a CGM frequency configuration to satisfy the
constraint while optimizing in the unconstrained dimension.
EdgeEngine uses Reinforcement Learning (RL) to adapt to the
changes in edge device characteristics induced by platform temperature variations (§4). EdgeEngine employs two domain-specific
heuristics that enable real-time adaptation when a temperature is
observed for the first time. These heuristics accelerate the adaptation process and minimize constraint violations. First, EdgeEngine
can identify and discard configurations that have a high probability
of violating constraints, thus preventing their selection. Second,
whenever EdgeEngine detects a constraint violation, it chooses a
frequency configuration that leads to efficient space exploration
while avoiding future violations.
Contributions. To the best of our knowledge, EdgeEngine is the first
thermal-aware frequency optimization framework for edge inference.
EdgeEngine incorporates real-time adaptation, and adjusts the frequency configuration of all the underlying components in response
to platform temperature changes during execution, without relying
on prior knowledge. Our contributions are:

Methodology

Temperature-Controlled Chamber. We place the Edge platform inside a temperature-controlled chamber (similar to [2]), which can
control the temperature and humidity conditions experienced by
the board during its operation. We systematically expose the board
to different ambient temperatures.
Environmental Setup. To create a controlled and stable environment for accurate data collection, the environmental setup involves
various steps to reduce experiment bias [23] (i.e., noise) including:
(i) maintaining the fan on all the time, (ii) CPU core isolation and
thread affinity, (iii) disabling concurrent user-space applications,
(iv) preventing I/O operations and remote access, and (v) enabling
PyTorch-specific optimizations at all times. We also make sure that
hardware throttling does not kick in.
Data Collection. To collect runtime and power data, we follow a
two-step procedure. First, we reboot the board, and run the target
benchmark five times to avoid cold start effects at both the application and board levels. Second, we repeatedly perform a sequence
that involves: (i) sending input from the CPU to the GPU, (ii) running the benchmark on the GPU, and (iii) sending the result back.
We refer to this sequence as a block. We use the block, rather than a
single benchmark execution, to eliminate the overhead associated
with the CUDA timers and synchronization. We determine the size
of each block based on two criteria: (i) it must have at least 100 iterations, and (ii) the CUDA runtime and synchronization overhead
should be less than 0.1% of the block runtime.
Terminology. We define a runtime observation as the average
benchmark execution time during a block, and a power observation
as the average power readings of onboard power monitors during
the block execution. We refer to a collection of observations as a
sample.
Non-Parametric Statistical Techniques. We ran Anderson-Darling
normality test [12] on runtime and power samples, and found that
the data does not follow a normal distribution. As a consequence,
we employ non-parametric statistical analysis. We determine the
stopping point for data collection based on observed values (similar
to [20]) such that our estimation of the median is robust. Specifically,
we define two stop criteria for collecting observations for a sample:
(i) the sample must contain at least 50 observations, and (ii) the
Relative Margin of Error (RME) for the estimation of the median
should be ≤ 0.5% with a confidence level of 99%.

FRAMEWORKS

Frequency optimization frameworks [8, 10, 11, 14] control the device’s frequency configuration and work on a two-dimensional constraint space: time and energy. For a given workload, the framework
will adjust the frequency configuration for some/all components
of the device (§3.1), to find a configuration that: (i) satisfies the
critical constraint dimension, and (ii) is efficient for the other unconstrained dimension (e.g., for a deadline-constrained application,
the frequency configuration must both meet the target deadline
and be energy efficient).
This section highlights the importance of taking into account
the effects of temperature on power and runtime in the context
of a frequency optimization framework (§3.2), and discusses the
limitations of existing thermal-oblivious frameworks in achieving
the objectives highlighted in §1. All experiments use the experimental setup described later in §5. The section starts by describing
the characteristics of the frequency configuration space (§3.1) and
concludes with a brief introduction to RL, the key technique we
use to support thermal-awareness (§4).

Unfortunately, all current optimization frameworks (Table 1) have
a common drawback - thermal obliviousness. Model-based nonadaptive frameworks are unable to perform well when facing platform temperature changes, as their models rely on information
collected at a fixed temperature. Section §3.2 has demonstrated
this for an optimal non-adaptive model-based framework. Figure
5 (and additional experiments in §6.1) highlight that even adaptive, frameworks perform sub-optimally when their design does
not take temperature into account. Figure 5 focuses on an state-ofthe-art adaptive, model-based (yet thermal-oblivious) framework:
NeuOS [7], specifically designed to meet deadlines for autonomous
vehicles using pre-characterized data. As the figure shows, NeuOS
fails to effectively adapt to the platform’s temperature changes. It
exhibits multiple instances of deadline violations when tested with
pre-populated data at 25°C and exposed to 70°C, highlighting its
inability to handle temperature variations.
On the other hand, adaptive model-free frameworks attempt to
adapt to constraint violations, including those caused by temperature changes without having any prior knowledge of the system.
However, these frameworks suffer from two drawbacks due to the
lack of platform temperature considerations in their design: (i) they
do not retain any knowledge of previously observed temperatures.
Consequently, each temperature change is treated as an entirely
new environment, requiring the adaptation process to start from
scratch. This limitation hinders their ability to achieve real-time
adaptation and results in a high number of violations (demonstrated
in Section 6.1). (ii) they may not adjust the frequency configuration
if the constraint is not violated. As a result, the frameworks may
settle for suboptimal configurations instead of actively seeking the
best configuration (demonstrated in Section 6.1).

We consider three main categories of objectives when designing
EdgeEngine (mapped to 𝑂 1−6 introduced in section 1).
1. High Accuracy Adaptation (𝑂 1−4 ). EdgeEngine must choose
the near-optimal frequency configurations with high accuracy at
execution time. Selecting an incorrect configuration may cause: (i)
constraint violations, which are unacceptable for real-time edge
inference, and/or (ii) a sub-optimal configuration that results in
higher energy consumption or lower performance.
EdgeEngine can adapt to new temperatures, without prior system knowledge (𝑂 1 ). When EdgeEngine encounters a new temperature for the first time, it initiates an exploration phase (detailed in Section 4.2). In this phase, it explores the effect of various frequency configurations by adjusting all CGM frequencies
to obtain near-optimal configuration (𝑂 2 ). To effectively address
the exploration-exploitation trade-off (Section 3.4), EdgeEngine
employs the epsilon-greedy policy [30]. It gradually reduces the
frequency of exploration actions and enters the exploitation phase
by selecting the previously determined near-optimal configuration.
The exploration phase, however, can result in numerous constraint violations, given the large configuration space (Section 3.1),
which is an intolerable outcome for several edge applications. To
address this issue, we propose two domain-specific heuristics (detailed in Section 4.2) based on our temperature characterization:
(i) action pruning and (ii) escape action. These enhancements give
EdgeEngine real-time adaptation capabilities (𝑂 3 ) by expediting
the exploration process, and minimizing the number of violations
incurred during exploration.
Even during the exploitation phase after EdgeEngine has selected
what appeared as a near-optimal configuration, variability in power
and runtime can lead to constraint violations. To mitigate this issue
(𝑂 4 ), EdgeEngine continuously monitors the inference metrics: if a
constraint violation is detected, EdgeEngine promptly adjusts the
frequency configuration to increase the guardband which protects
it against variability in runtime or power.

Epsilon ’decay’ can be applied, gradually reducing the exploration

2. Lightweight Design (𝑂 5 ). EdgeEngine should impose minimal
latency overhead to avoid increasing the ML inference time. It
should also have minimal computation and memory overheads to
be compatible with resource-constrained edge platforms. To minimize latency overhead, we employ Q-Learning instead of alternative
RL techniques such as Deep RL (DRL) [21] or Temporal Difference
Learning (TD Learning) [27]. Q-Learning’s utilization of a lookup
table called the Q-Table allows for efficient action selection with
minimal latency. This lightweight algorithm also requires limited
computational resources [17], making it well-suited for our objective. Furthermore, we ensure EdgeEngine has minimal memory
overhead, by storing the Q-Table in compact format.

APPROACH: EDGEENGINE

We propose EdgeEngine (Figure 6), a thermal-aware optimization
framework for edge inference. EdgeEngine uses a lightweight RL
technique (Q-Learning [27]) (§3.4) to meet the application’s objectives and adapt to temperature changes. Based on the application’s
constraints and the platform’s temperature, EdgeEngine optimizes
the frequency configuration of the CGM components.
EdgeEngine can adapt to two dimensions of application constraints: deadline and energy constraints. Users set the application’s
constraint dimension with the maximum permissible value. For
an energy-constrained application, they set the maximum energy
budget per inference, and for a deadline-constrained application,
users set the target deadline for each inference. The RL agent selects
a configuration that satisfies the constraint and optimizes on the
unconstrained dimension.

3. Generalizability (𝑂 6 ). EdgeEngine aims to be versatile and compatible with diverse environments, applications, and constraints.
The model-free nature of Q-Learning [26], enables EdgeEngine
to adapt seamlessly to different environments and applications.
Unlike methods that rely on pre-existing knowledge about system
dynamics [7, 8, 10, 11], Q-Learning learns directly from interactions
with the environment, enabling EdgeEngine to dynamically adjust
its behavior and configuration based on its specific operational
context.

5.1

RL Parameters Specific to EdgeEngine

Hyperparameters. To determine the optimal values for the hyperparameters learning rate (𝛼) and discount factor (𝛾), we conduct a
sensitivity analysis. We consider three classes of values for each
parameter: low (0.2), medium (0.5), and high (0.8). EdgeEngine is
being trained on Mobilenetv2 using all nine combinations of these
values, resulting in a total of nine experiments. Through our analysis, we observe that the agent always performs most effectively
when the learning rate is set to a high value and the discount factor
to a low value.
Exploration Rate. We set the maximum 𝜖 value as 0.995, and
the minimum value as 0.01. We also use the epsilon decay technique
by multiplying by the maximum 𝜖 value at the end of each round.
This approach allows for a gradual reduction of exploration and an
increased focus on exploitation as the experiment progresses.
State Quantization. EdgeEngine state is determined by the
current CGM Frequency level and temperature. We use the Nvidia
Jetson TX2 frequency levels: 12, 11, and 13 frequency levels for
CPU, GPU, and memory controller respectively [28]. We discreteize
the operating temperature range of Jetson TX2 [29] (-25ºC to 80ºC)
into 21 levels, each corresponding to a 5ºC temperature range.

An action is prohibited in a specific CGM if it previously caused
violations at the current temperature or at lower temperatures.
This prohibition mechanism is based on the characterization study
(§2) that shows that increasing the temperature leads to higher
runtime and energy consumption. This action pruning leads to
decreasing the search space size, as EdgeEngine observes more
temperatures, which leads to faster adaptation, and fewer violations
during exploration (we evaluate these improvements in Section 6.2).
Escape Action: In the event of a constraint violation, EdgeEngine
promptly adjusts the frequency configuration to bring it closer to
the optimal configuration (line 16), reducing the likelihood of future
violations. For applications with deadline constraints, EdgeEngine
increases the frequency configuration by two levels rather than
one, if a deadline is missed. This approach is based on our observation that increasing the frequency generally leads to performance
improvement.
However, our characterization study reveals that the impact on
energy consumption is not as straightforward. In some cases, increasing the frequency leads to higher energy consumption, while
in others, it has the opposite effect. This is because energy consumption depends on the product of power and runtime, and the
influence of frequency changes on these factors is in opposite directions. Therefore, we do not perform this optimization for energyconstrained applications.

EVALUATION

This section presents the experimental evaluation of EdgeEngine
in the context of different constraint scenarios (§5.5) and applications (§5.3). First, we focus on EdgeEngine’s ability to satisfy the
applications’ constraints and meet the optimization objective as
temperature changes (§6.1). We evaluate EdgeEngine’s efficiency by
comparing it to an idealized oracle. We highlight the importance of
thermal-awareness by comparing EdgeEngine with state-of-the-art
frequency optimization frameworks, all thermal-oblivious as well
as with thermal-oblivious versions of EdgeEngine. Finally, we assess EdgeEngine’s practicality (§6.2) by examining how it satisfies
thermal-aware framework objectives (𝑂 1−6 ).

Adaptability and Efficiency

We assess EdgeEngine’s adaptability to different temperatures by
evaluating two metrics: the constraint violation rate, and the quality
of the solution in the unconstrained dimension.
To conduct the experiment, we use the following temperature
variation scenario: Initially, we set the ambient temperature to 25°C
and incrementally increase it to 65°C, subsequently decreasing it
back to 25°C. At each interval of 10°C, we allow for a stable temperature period of 500 inferences before changing the temperature
again. During the experiment, we record the number of violations,
and, at the end of the experiment, we estimate the runtime and
energy consumption of the final configuration for each temperature
level.
Our chosen methodology aims to thoroughly test the adaptability of EdgeEngine across different temperature ranges, both when
encountering them for the first time (on temperature increase)
and when already observed them (on temperature decrease). This
methodology also evaluates EdgeEngine in a challenging environment where temperatures fluctuate rapidly, with each 10ºC temperature increase or decrease in 5 seconds. However, we also conducted
tests in more relaxed environments where temperature changes
occur gradually. In these scenarios, we observed that EdgeEngine
exhibited improved performance as the temperature evolved more
slowly. This favorable outcome can be attributed to EdgeEngine’s
thermal awareness and embedded heuristics, which enable it to
adapt and optimize its operations accordingly.

Overheads. We estimate EdgeEngine runtime and memory overhead to show it is practical for deployment in resource-constrained
environments (𝑂 5 ). Note that our Python-based implementation
of EdgeEngine is not optimized - thus our estimates are an upper
bound of overheads in a realistic deployment. The runtime overhead
of EdgeEngine on Jetson TX2, on average across all the models,
is 340𝜇s. This overhead is the total execution time of selecting
an action, calculating the reward, and updating the Q-table. Compared to the Squeezenet execution time (13ms), the fastest model
in our experiment, this overhead constitutes 2.61% of the total inference latency. Regarding memory overheard, the memory usage
of EdgeEngine is 16MB, on average, over the applications. This is
significantly lower than the memory footprint of the applications,
which often use GBs of data.
To conclude, EdgeEngine can swiftly adapt to temperature
changes (𝑂 1 ) and runtime noise (𝑂 3 ) by adjusting all components
(𝑂 2 ). It also exhibits real-time adaptation capabilities by having a
low number of violations when it observes a temperature for the
first time (𝑂 4 ). EdgeEngine has the above ability across temperatures, applications, and constraints (𝑂 5 ) and maintains low runtime
and memory overheads (𝑂 6 ).

Why not using deep reinforcement learning instead of QLearning? The key challenge with employing modern deep reinforcement learning models [18] for adapting to constraints is their
substantial computational demands, rendering them impractical
for resource-constrained edge platforms. For instance, both Herti
[14] and zTT [16] rely on deep reinforcement learning but follow a
training approach that occurs on a separate server, with the trained
model subsequently transferred to the edge platform. This method
lacks real-time adaptability, as it can take 30-183 milliseconds to
determine the optimal action for zTT (significantly more compared
to EdgeEngine). Furthermore, the adaptation process, involving the
training of the deep Q-Network, is relatively slow, surpassing 300s
for zTT, while EdgeEngine completes this process in just 15s.

SUMMARY

We demonstrate the effects of temperature variation on current
thermal-oblivious frequency optimization frameworks showing
that they either violate the application’s constraints or select
performance/energy-inefficient configurations. To address this
challenge, we present EdgeEngine, a lightweight optimization
framework that adapts to the environment’s temperature and the
application’s constraints using RL. Compared to thermal-oblivious
frameworks, EdgeEngine (i) selects frequency configurations that
satisfy the applications’ constraints, (ii) reduces violations by an
average of 41% with an 8% lower runtime for energy-constrained
applications, and (iii) reduces violations by 26%, with 29% lower
energy consumption for deadline-constrained applications.
